How TrackShift BMS Works
The physics, math, and decision logic behind every number on this dashboard.

1. Vehicle Model — Reduced-Order Point Mass

The car is modeled as a point mass with no heading or 2D position during the race itself (only distance travelled along the track) — a deliberate simplification that keeps the simulation fast enough to run hundreds of what-if races (baselines, Oracle, Monte Carlo) in well under a second.

Fdrag = 0.5 · ρ · CdA · v²
Fdownforce = 0.5 · ρ · ClA · v²
Froll = Crr · (m·g + Fdownforce)

Standard drag/downforce equations. Rolling resistance scales with total normal force — vehicle weight plus aerodynamic downforce pressing the tires into the track, so a high-downforce setup also rolls with more resistance.

Ftraction_max = μlong · (m·g + Fdownforce)
Fdrive = min( Pavailable / v, Ftraction_max )
a = (Fdrive − Fdrag − Froll) / m

Drive force is capped by whichever is smaller: how much force the requested power can produce at the current speed (P/v), or how much force the tires can physically put down before losing grip. This is why raw power alone can't accelerate a stationary car instantly — at v→0, P/v is enormous but gets clamped hard by the traction ceiling.

vcorner_max = √⁠ ( (μlat·m·g) / (m/r − μlat·0.5·ρ·ClA) )

Cornering speed limit, derived from balancing lateral tire grip (weight plus downforce-assisted) against the centripetal force a corner of radius r demands. Rain scales the effective μ down by ~20%, dropping this limit by roughly √0.64 ≈ 80% of dry-grip cornering speed.

2. Battery Model

SOC(t+1) = SOC(t) − Edischarge + ηregen · Eregen

Discharge is modeled as lossless; regen applies a round-trip efficiency (90% by default) — recovering 1 MJ of kinetic energy stores 0.9 MJ. Regenerated energy is derived directly from kinetic-energy conservation (½·m·v² before vs. after), not from a separately-computed braking force — this guarantees the car's kinetic energy always drops by exactly what gets credited to the battery, regardless of how finely a time step is subdivided internally.

Both discharge power and regen power are capped by the regulatory deployment curve and MGU-K regen limit respectively; SOC is always clamped to [0, capacity].

3. Energy Forecaster

Predicts how much energy (Ê) is still needed to finish the race, with an explicit uncertainty band (σÊ):

  • First ~100m of a race — no telemetry history yet, so this falls back to a physics-based prior: expected drag + rolling-resistance rate, net of a typical per-meter regen credit.
  • After ~100m — blends the cumulative net-energy rate observed so far (for stability) with how much the recent rate has been diverging from it (for uncertainty) — a sudden change in driving conditions (rain, traffic, a technical section) widens σÊ immediately.

Both branches are floored at zero — a physically valid energy requirement can never be negative, i.e. the car can never be predicted to net gain energy over the remaining race.

Rsafety(t) = Rbase + k · σÊ(t) + Revent(t)

The strategic reserve every policy holds back before it's willing to spend battery. R_base = 0.5 MJ is a fixed floor; k = 2.0 (≈95% confidence under a normal-distribution assumption) scales the uncertainty term; R_event adds live-disturbance contingency — +0.15 MJ while a safety car is active (pace resumes unpredictably once it ends), +0.10 MJ in the rain (noisier lap-to-lap consumption), +0.05 MJ under limited-power mode (a lower ceiling makes any shortfall harder to recover from). This is the same formula the Oracle, the live MPC, and every baseline policy are held to — the whole "% of Oracle performance" comparison only means something if everyone plays by the same reserve rule.

4. Proposed System — Receding-Horizon MPC

The live strategy driving the car each tick. Re-solves a short lookahead (5 steps) every single time step, rather than committing to a fixed plan — reacting immediately to rain, safety cars, or a corner appearing sooner than expected.

  • Corner-approach braking — before every step it checks the next corner: if within braking distance ((v² − v_limit²) / (2·a_brake_max), plus a safety margin) of needing to slow down, it requests full regen instead of running the optimizer at all.
  • Deployable check — Deployable = SOC − Ê − R_safety. Once this drops to zero, deployment is forced off entirely (hard conservation) rather than gradually tapered.
  • Water-filling allocation — otherwise, the energy budget available over the horizon is spread as evenly as possible across every step, except where a step's own usefulness ceiling is lower (see below) — in which case that step is pinned at its ceiling and the freed-up budget is redistributed across the remaining steps. This converges in at most horizon_steps passes and always uses the full available budget.
  • Usefulness ceiling — deploying more power than a step can actually turn into speed is pure waste. Each step's ceiling accounts for traction headroom above what the ICE alone already demands, whether the car is already at its current segment's corner-speed cap, and whether it's approaching a corner it would just get clamped at anyway.

Why not just deploy everything as early as possible? Because F = P/v means the speed benefit of extra power shrinks as speed rises — a big early burst reaches a high peak speed briefly then leaves nothing for the rest of the lap, while the same energy spread across the whole race raises cruising speed the entire way. Measured head-to-head with identical total energy, the spread allocation finished a lap several seconds faster than front-loading.

5. Oracle — Theoretical Best

Not a controller — a single offline solve over the entire remaining race with zero forecast uncertainty (perfect future knowledge), computed once and replayed. Uses the exact same water-filling allocator as the live MPC, just over the whole race horizon instead of a 5-step window, which is what makes it a meaningful ceiling to measure the MPC against rather than a different algorithm entirely.

6. Baseline Policies

Four deliberately simple strategies the Proposed System and Oracle are benchmarked against — every one of them still respects the same 0.5 MJ hard reserve floor, so the comparison is apples-to-apples:

  • Baseline 0 — No Optimization: full deployment until the reserve floor, then switches to full regen.
  • Baseline 1 — Aggressive: full deployment whenever above the hard floor, ignoring the strategic reserve headroom a cautious policy would hold back.
  • Baseline 2 — Conservative: capped at a fixed fraction (default 60%) of max deployment power at all times.
  • Baseline 3 — Fixed Heuristic: a flat 70% of max deployment power, a simple "set it and forget it" schedule.

7. Procedural Track Generation

Each track class (Balanced, Technical, High-Speed, …) is a probability distribution over segment types — straights, high-speed/medium/slow corners, hairpins — sampled until the target length is reached. Two things keep the result meaningful rather than arbitrary:

  • Feasibility check — the candidate lap is walked once assuming the vehicle brakes as late as physically possible into every corner; any track where a corner can't be reached from the previous segment's achievable speed within the available braking distance is rejected and resampled.
  • Closed-loop closure — a real return path (two same-direction corners plus a connecting straight, solved as a restricted Dubins path) bends the track from wherever the random walk ends back to the exact start/finish pose, so the circuit the 3D view and minimap draw is an actual closed loop, not a cosmetic line connecting two open ends.

8. Simulation Engine

Each simulated time step (1 second of race time by default) is internally subdivided into substeps of at most 0.1s before the Euler-integration update is applied — the vehicle's own dynamics move fast enough (50+ m/s² under full traction near a standing start) that a single 1-second jump would be a crude approximation of the true curve. This is purely an internal accuracy improvement; a caller requesting a 1-second step still gets exactly 1 second of advanced race time and identical cumulative-energy accounting.

Every race step follows the same loop: the active policy (MPC / Oracle / a baseline) decides a deploy/regen request → the simulator advances the physics → the result (speed, SOC, lap, strategy state) streams to this dashboard over a websocket, at whatever playback-speed multiplier is selected.

9. Overtake Opportunity Assessment

On straights longer than 400m, evaluates the energy cost of a hypothetical +15 km/h overtaking burst (drag/rolling-resistance power over the burst distance, plus a fixed MGU-K override cost) against currently deployable energy, and classifies the risk as LOW (attack), MARGINAL (driver discretion), or HIGH (hold) accordingly.
